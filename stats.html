Batting stats

Contact

AVG Batting Average

Higher is better

Hits divided by at bats.

A .300 average means three hits in every ten at bats. Walks, hit by pitch and sacrifices are not at bats, so they neither help nor hurt the average. Written baseball style without the leading zero: .345, not 0.345. Youth averages run higher than professional ones because fielding is weaker, so the performance targets on the charts are set for your player's age division rather than borrowed from the pros.

H ÷ AB

OBP On-Base Percentage

Higher is better

How often the batter reaches base by a hit, a walk or being hit by a pitch.

A walk counts the same as a single here, because a runner on first is a runner on first. OBP rewards patience at the plate in a way batting average cannot. The textbook version also adds sacrifice flies to the bottom of the fraction, since a sacrifice fly is an out. GameChanger prints one combined sacrifice number without separating bunts from flies, so Full Count leaves sacrifices out of the calculation entirely. At youth levels the difference is tiny.

(H + BB + HBP) ÷ (AB + BB + HBP)

SLG Slugging Percentage

Higher is better

Total bases divided by at bats.

It measures power: a batter whose hits go for extra bases slugs higher than one who hits singles. A single is one base, a double two, a triple three and a home run four. A .500 slugging percentage means half a base per at bat. Slugging can be above 1.000, since a home run is worth four bases in one at bat.

OPS On-Base Plus Slugging

Higher is better

On-base percentage added to slugging percentage.

One number that covers both getting on base and hitting for power. OPS is a quick way to compare hitters when you do not want to look at two numbers. It adds two fractions with different denominators, which statisticians grumble about, but it tracks run production well. Above 1.000 is exceptional at any level.

OBP + SLG

AB At Bats

Neither is better

Trips to the plate that ended in a hit or an out, not counting walks, hit by pitch or sacrifices.

Neither high nor low is better. It is the number AVG and SLG divide by. A plate appearance is every trip to the plate; an at bat is only the ones that ended with the batter putting the ball in play or striking out. A batter who walks twice and singles once in three trips has one at bat and one hit, a 1.000 average. That is why rate stats need a season to mean anything.

Production

H Hits

Higher is better

Times the batter reached base safely on a batted ball, without an error or a fielder's choice.

Hits are the most direct way a batter helps the team score. Singles, doubles, triples and home runs all count as one hit each. Reaching base on an error is not a hit, and neither is a walk, which is why AVG and OBP tell different stories about the same player.

RBI Runs Batted In

Higher is better

Runs that scored because of what the batter did at the plate.

RBI show how often the batter came through with runners on. A hit, a walk with the bases loaded, a sacrifice fly and a ground out that scores a runner all earn an RBI. A run that scores on an error or during a double play does not. RBI depend heavily on who bats ahead of the player, so a good hitter low in the order can have few of them.

R Runs Scored

Higher is better

Times the player crossed home plate.

Runs are the point of the game, and a player who gets on base and runs well scores a lot of them. A run counts however the player reached base and however they came home, whether driven in by a teammate, on a steal of home or on an error. It rewards getting on base and good baserunning as much as hitting.

TB Total Bases

Higher is better

Bases reached on hits: one for a single, two for a double, three for a triple, four for a home run.

It gives extra-base hits the extra credit a plain hit count does not. Bases reached by walks, steals or errors are not total bases; only hits count. Full Count reads TB from the note printed under the box score rather than working it out, so it matches what GameChanger shows.

RC Runs Created

Higher is better

An estimate of how many of the team's runs this player's batting produced.

It rolls hits, walks and power into one number in the units that matter: runs. Bill James's original formula from the 1970s, and the calculation Full Count uses unless you change it. The idea is that runs come from two things: getting on base, and moving runners along. The first part counts how often the batter reached base, the second counts how far their hits went, and dividing by chances keeps a player who bats more often from running away with it. Hit by pitch and stolen bases are not in this version, so a player who gets hit a lot is undercounted a little. Over a season, a team's Runs Created adds up close to its actual runs. For one game it swings with two or three trips to the plate, so the season and career figures are the ones to read. Settings offers a second calculation, Weighted, described under wRC; the Runs Created tile and chart show whichever one you choose.

(H + BB) × TB ÷ (AB + BB)

wRC Weighted Runs Created

Higher is better

Runs Created with each way of reaching base worth what it actually produced in this player's games, compared with the other hitters in those games.

It is the Weighted choice for Runs Created in Settings; the Runs Created tile and chart show it once you pick it. The major leagues measure how many runs a walk, a single, a double, a triple and a home run are each worth, and credit every hitter at those values instead of treating a walk like a single or a double like two singles. Full Count does the same, but with numbers from your player's own games rather than MLB's. Every box score you import holds every player's line, both teams. Full Count adds up each team's singles, doubles, triples, home runs, walks, hit batters and outs for each game, next to the runs that team scored, and works out the run value of each event that best explains those runs on these fields, with an out allowed to cost runs. Those values weight your player's line, and the result is compared with what an average hitter in the same games would have produced over the same trips to the plate. Nothing from MLB is used. Because youth teams hit few triples and fewer home runs, a home run is never allowed to be worth less than a triple, or a triple less than a double, and the calculation waits until about ten games with full box scores are in before it shows a number; before that the tile shows -- and the chart explains why. Games imported before September 2026 gain their rosters automatically the next time the app opens, from the files it kept.

wOBA = (w1B × 1B + w2B × 2B + w3B × 3B + wHR × HR + wBB × BB + wHBP × HBP) ÷ (AB + BB + HBP)
wRC = (wOBA − average wOBA in these games + runs per PA in these games) × PA

Discipline

BB Walks

Higher is better

Times the batter was sent to first base on four balls.

A walk is a runner on base without risking an out. BB stands for base on balls. Walks show a batter who knows the strike zone and does not chase. They count toward on-base percentage but not batting average.

SO Strikeouts

Lower is better

Plate appearances that ended on three strikes.

A strikeout is an out on which nothing could happen: no runner moved, no fielder had to make a play. Written as SO on the box score and often as K in conversation. Raw strikeouts grow with plate appearances, so the charts use K Rate to compare games of different length.

K Rate Strikeout Rate

Lower is better

The share of plate appearances that ended in a strikeout.

Under 20% is a good target for younger players and under 15% for older ones. Plate appearances here are at bats plus walks, hit by pitch and sacrifices, so every trip to the plate counts. A rate lets a two-trip game and a four-trip game share a chart.

SO ÷ PA × 100

BB Rate Walk Rate

Higher is better

The share of plate appearances that ended in a walk.

Around 15% is a strong rate for a youth hitter. The counterpart to K Rate. Together they describe plate discipline: a batter with a high walk rate and a low strikeout rate is controlling the at bat rather than being controlled by the pitcher.

Bunts or fly balls that made an out on purpose to move a runner along or score one.

Neither high nor low is better. A sacrifice is a team play the coach called for, not a measure of the hitter. A sacrifice does not count as an at bat, so it does not lower the batting average. Baserunning

SB Stolen Bases

Higher is better

Bases taken by running while the pitcher was delivering, without a hit or an error.

A steal moves a runner into scoring position without using an out. Steals are common in youth softball, where catchers' arms are still developing, so a fast player can rack up a lot of them. Compare with Caught Stealing to see whether the running is paying off.

CS Caught Stealing

Lower is better

Steal attempts that ended with the runner tagged out.

Each one gives away a runner who was already on base. An out on the bases costs more than most outs at the plate, because the runner had already done the hard part. A few are the price of running aggressively; a lot of them means the running is costing the team.

SB% Stolen Base Percentage

Higher is better

The share of steal attempts that succeeded.

Above 75% the running is helping the team; below that the outs cost more than the bases gain. Full Count shows a dash until the first steal attempt of the season. In youth ball success rates run high, so a player well under 75% is being sent when they should not be, or is leaving too late.

Pitching stats

Efficiency

Strike % Strike Percentage

Higher is better

The share of all pitches thrown that were strikes.

A pitcher who throws strikes gets ahead in the count, keeps walks down and makes batters swing. Every strike counts: called, swinging, foul balls and balls hit into play. Full Count reads pitches and strikes from the Pitches-Strikes line printed under the box score. If that line was not captured, the game shows no strike percentage at all rather than a misleading 0%. Around 60% is a solid target for youth pitchers, and the charts draw the target lines for your player's age division.

Strikes ÷ Pitches × 100

WHIP Walks and Hits per Inning Pitched

Lower is better

How many runners a pitcher puts on base per inning, by walk or by hit.

A WHIP of 1.0 means about one runner reaches base every inning. WHIP measures what the pitcher gave up directly. Hit batters and runners who reached on an error are not included. Innings are counted in thirds, so an outing written as 4.1 is four and one third innings. Because it is a rate, one short outing can swing it a long way; the season figure is the one to trust.

(BB + H) ÷ IP

Run Prevention

ERA Earned Runs per Inning

Lower is better

Earned runs allowed divided by innings pitched.

It tells you how many runs the pitcher gives up through their own pitching, rather than through errors behind them. A traditional ERA multiplies this by the length of a full game: seven innings in softball, nine in the major leagues. Youth games run four, five or six innings, so Full Count leaves the number per inning instead of assuming a game length. That makes it smaller than the ERA you hear on television. Here, 0.50 means one earned run every two innings, and 1.00 means one every inning. At the youngest ages most runs score on errors and are unearned, so the performance targets for ERA begin at 12U.

ER ÷ IP

RS Run Support

Neither is better

The average number of runs the team scored in the games the pitcher threw from start to finish.

Neither direction is a mark on the pitcher: it measures the help the offense gave the pitcher, which the pitcher does not control. Higher is easier to pitch with. Only games the pitcher threw from the first pitch to the last count, so the figure describes the offense behind a complete outing. A game with another pitcher on the team is left out entirely, and so is a game with no final score. It is per game, not per inning, and the Rate Stats setting does not change it. A game imported from a box score, by PDF or screenshots, carries both the score and who pitched; a game that came only from a CSV has neither.

Team runs in the games the pitcher threw whole ÷ those games

From box scores only: it needs the innings line score.

ER Earned Runs

Lower is better

Runs that scored without help from an error or a passed ball.

These are the runs the pitcher is held responsible for. The scorekeeper decides which runs are earned by working out how the inning would have gone with no errors. That judgment is only as careful as the scorekeeping, so the split between earned and unearned runs is more reliable in older divisions. Comparing ER to Runs Allowed shows how much the defense cost the pitcher.

From box scores and CSV exports.

Lower is better

Every run that scored while this pitcher was in the game, earned or not.

This is the number that decides the game, whoever was at fault. Runs Allowed includes runs that scored on errors, passed balls and anything else. A pitcher with far more runs allowed than earned runs is being let down by the fielding, which is common and worth knowing before judging the pitching.

Fewer hits means fewer runners and fewer runs. Any hit counts the same here, whether a single or a home run. A ball that a fielder should have caught and did not is scored as an error, not a hit, so this number is about what the batter earned.

Command & Contact

K per Inning Strikeouts per Inning

Higher is better

Strikeouts divided by innings pitched.

At 1.0, one of every three outs is a strikeout. A four-inning outing and a six-inning outing cannot be compared on raw strikeouts, but they can be compared per inning. That is why the charts plot this rather than the total. A strikeout is an out the fielders did not have to make, which matters most while fielding is still developing.

Reliability

FPCT Fielding Percentage

Higher is better

The share of a player's fielding chances handled without an error.

A player at .950 makes the play nineteen times out of twenty. Read it with the position in mind. An infielder or a catcher handles harder plays and runs lower than an outfielder at the same age, so the same number can be routine at one position and excellent at another. Full Count shows one figure for all the positions a player played, since the CSV gives chances only as a total. Nothing is shown for a player with no chances, because a player who touched nothing is not a 1.000 fielder. Full Count reads fielding and catching from GameChanger CSV exports; box score screenshots and PDFs do not carry them.

(PO + A) ÷ (PO + A + E)

Plays a fielder should have made with ordinary effort and did not.

Every error hands the other team a base or an out they had not earned. The scorekeeper decides what counts as an error, and youth scorekeeping is generous both ways. A hard-hit ball that gets through is usually a hit, not an error. Control

CS % Caught Stealing Percentage

Higher is better

The share of stolen base attempts against the catcher that ended with the runner thrown out.

It is the clearest measure of a catcher's arm and release. Full Count shows the rate only once the catcher has faced ten attempts; below that the tile shows the counts instead, because two of five is 40 percent and says nothing. Expect it to read low in youth ball: college catchers throw out about one runner in six, and at 10U and 12U a stolen base is often a passed ball or a wild pitch that nobody could have thrown on. The performance targets take that into account. Full Count reads catching stats from GameChanger CSV exports.

PB Passed Balls

Lower is better

Pitches the catcher should have stopped with ordinary effort and did not, on which a runner advanced.

It is the catcher's own error column. A pitch the catcher had no fair chance at is a wild pitch and goes against the pitcher instead. The scorekeeper draws that line. Read the count against innings caught, shown beside it: two passed balls in a full game and two in a two-inning stint are not the same afternoon.

INN Innings Caught

Neither is better

Innings the player spent behind the plate.

Neither is better. It is the workload every catching rate is measured against. Counted in thirds like innings pitched. In the CSV this is the INN column of the Fielding section, which equals the C column of innings by position.

CS Caught Stealing

Higher is better

Runners the catcher threw out trying to steal.

Each one is a base runner erased. This is the catcher's side of the number. The batting page has a Caught Stealing of its own, which counts the times this player was thrown out as a runner. The box score's CS note describes the runner, not the catcher, which is why the catcher's count comes only from the CSV.

SB Steals Allowed

Lower is better

Stolen bases the other team took while the player was catching.

Lower is better, but read it against attempts. A team that runs a lot will run up this number on any catcher. Youth scorekeepers often score a runner advancing on a passed ball or a wild pitch as a stolen base, so this count includes plays the catcher never had a throw on. Attempts and CS % together say more than this number alone.

SBATT Stolen Base Attempts

Neither is better

Every time a runner tried to steal while the player was catching: steals allowed plus runners caught.

Neither is better. It says how often the catcher was tested. It is the denominator of CS %, and the reason that rate waits for ten attempts before it is shown.

PIK Pickoffs

Higher is better

Runners the catcher picked off a base with a throw.

They are rare and they change innings. A pickoff is a snap throw behind a runner who has drifted too far off the base, as opposed to throwing out a runner in the act of stealing. Shown as a count; it is too rare for a rate.

Neither is better

Games in which the player caught at least one out.

Neither is better. It is the count behind every catching total. Only games imported from a GameChanger CSV export carry a catching line, and only when the player actually caught. A game played at another position has no catching line at all.
